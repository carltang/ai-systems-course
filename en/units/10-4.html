Part 10 · Reinforcement learning (optional)10.4 How industry uses reinforcement learning

Translated by AI (Anthropic's Claude) from the Chinese edition. The Chinese original is the reference.

This unit answersWhere is reinforcement learning mainly used in industry today? How is it related to large models?
Follows 10.1–10.3: with the principles and the systems as a foundation, this unit looks at where it is actually used.

The thread: first its two points of contact with large models, one using human preferences as the reward, the other using verifiable results as the reward → then its traditional strength: robots and control → then a few classic application areas → finally, a practical question: faced with a new task, how do you judge whether to use reinforcement learning?

  1. Aligning large models: human preferences as the reward (RLHF)
  2. Training reasoning ability: verifiable results as the reward
  3. Robots and control: train in simulation, transfer to real machines
  4. Games, recommendation, scheduling: a few classic application areas
  5. What problems suit reinforcement learning

Explanation

  1. 1Aligning large models: human preferences as the reward (RLHF)

    Pretraining teaches a language model "what word is most likely next", but "most likely" is not the same as "what people want". RLHF turns human judgement into a training signal in three steps: first have people compare different answers from the model and mark which is better; use these comparisons to train a reward model that learns to score answers; then use reinforcement learning to adjust the language model so that its answers score highly with the reward model.

    Why pretraining is not enough. The language model training of part 1 is maximum likelihood: make the model give high probability to the next word in real text. The training data is all kinds of writing from the internet. Such a model learns language and knowledge, but its goal is "to resemble the training data", not "to be helpful, honest and harmless". The latter is hard to write down as a formula, and there is no ready-made dataset to imitate directly.

    This is exactly the situation 10.1 described as suiting reinforcement learning: you cannot say how it should be done, but once it is done you can judge whether it was good.

    Write it as a reinforcement learning problem.

    • Agent: the language model.
    • State: the user's question, plus what has been generated so far.
    • Action: the next token.
    • An episode: generating one complete answer.
    • Reward: once the answer is written, one score for the whole answer.

    A language model's output is a probability distribution over the next token anyway, so it is naturally a policy (10.2).

    The difficulty: who gives the reward. The ideal reward is "whether a person finds this answer good". But reinforcement learning needs tens of thousands of pieces of feedback, having people score every answer is too slow and too expensive, and the absolute scores people give are very inconsistent.

    Three steps (as in the InstructGPT paper).

    • Step one: supervised fine-tuning. First collect a batch of high-quality demonstration answers written by people, and fine-tune the pretrained model on them with ordinary supervised learning, giving a reasonable starting point.
    • Step two: train a reward model. For the same question, have the model generate several different answers and ask people to compare them: which is better. Comparing is easier and more consistent than giving absolute scores. Use this comparison data to train another model: input a question and an answer, output a score, such that answers people judged better score higher.
    • Step three: reinforcement learning. Use PPO to adjust the language model: have it generate answers, score them with the reward model, and update by the methods of 10.2, so that it leans towards generating high-scoring answers.

    Step three has one more important constraint: the adjusted model is not allowed to stray too far from the original (a penalty term is added to the objective). The reason is the same as PPO limiting its step size, and matters even more here: the reward model is reliable only on the kinds of answer it has seen. If the language model is optimised out of shape, it may find outputs that the reward model "thinks are very good" but are actually nonsense.

    This is the reward hacking of 10.1. The reward model is an approximation of human preferences, and not a perfect one. The language model optimises this approximation, not people's real preferences. The harder it optimises, the more easily it exploits holes in the approximation. In practice this shows up as answers getting long-winded (the reward model favours long answers), pandering to users too much, and covering uncertainty with a confident-sounding tone.

    From the systems point of view. By the analysis at the end of 10.3: the collection phase is batched generation by the large model (9.1, 9.3), plus inference by the reward model; the learning phase is backpropagation through the large model (part 7). Each round's data is used only a few times and thrown away (PPO is on-policy). It is a job that joins an inference system to a training system.

    Later developments. One family of methods (DPO, for example) bypasses the explicit reward model and the reinforcement learning loop, building a supervised-style loss directly from preference comparison data; it is much simpler to implement and is now widely used too. They solve the same problem: how to adjust a model using "which one people prefer".

    An analogyLike training a new chef: first invite diners to taste several versions of a dish and say which they prefer; sum up the diners' tastes into a "taster"; after that, every new dish the chef makes is judged by this taster, without inviting the diners in every time.

    So the key to RLHF lies not in the reinforcement learning algorithm (it is just PPO) but in where the reward comes from: human preferences are expensive, so a model is learned to score in people's place. It is what makes products like ChatGPT "pleasant to use", and it also brings all the problems of an unreliable reward model.

  2. RLHF's reward comes from subjective human preference, and is both expensive and imprecise. For some tasks the reward can come much more cleanly.

  3. 2Training reasoning ability: verifiable results as the reward

    For one kind of task, a program can judge automatically whether the result is right: the answer to a maths problem, whether code passes its tests, whether a proof of a theorem holds. Then neither people nor a learned reward model is needed: 1 for right, 0 for wrong. Let the model try all kinds of ways of solving the problem itself, and whenever it ends up right, reinforce that whole chain of thinking. This is one of the main ways the "reasoning" models of the past two years have been trained.

    The setting. The language model is still treated as a policy. Give it a problem, have it write out its thinking step by step, and finally give an answer. Then a program checks the answer:

    • Maths problems: compare with the reference answer.
    • Programming problems: run the code and see whether it passes all the test cases.
    • Formal proofs: verify each step with a proof checker.
    • More general "agent" tasks: have the model operate an environment (browsing web pages, using tools, modifying a code repository), and finally check whether the goal was reached.

    The reward is given only at the end: right or wrong.

    This is the extreme case of 10.1's delayed reward. The model writes thousands of tokens of thinking and receives a single number only at the end. Which intermediate steps helped and which were detours gets no direct feedback at all. The policy gradient approach (10.2) is: for the attempts that got it right, push up the probability of every token in them; for those that got it wrong, push it down. Sample many different attempts at the same problem, compare the right ones with the wrong ones, and the really useful lines of thought emerge statistically.

    Compared with RLHF.

    RLHFVerifiable rewards
    Reward comes fromA learned reward model (approximating human preferences)Automatic judgement by a program
    Reliability of the rewardHas holes, gets exploitedHigh (provided the judging program is correct)
    Cost of the rewardCollecting human comparisons is expensiveAlmost free, can be done in vast numbers
    GoalAnswers that match people's tastesGetting the model to solve problems correctly
    Can it surpass existing demonstrationsLimited by human judgementYes

    The last row is the key. Supervised learning can only make a model imitate solutions people have written. With verifiable rewards, the model can find its own ways of solving problems that nobody ever wrote down, as long as it gets the right result in the end. It is the same principle as AlphaGo surpassing human players through self-play (10.1).

    The reward can still be exploited. "Verifiable" does not mean "watertight":

    • The test cases are incomplete, and the model writes code that handles only those cases.
    • The judging program has a bug, and the model learns to trigger the bug instead of solving the problem.
    • In agent tasks, the model modifies the tests directly, or reaches the apparent goal in a way that is not allowed.

    So in designing such training, a great deal of the work goes into constructing problems and making the judging strict.

    Heavier on the systems side. Compared with RLHF:

    • Each sample has to generate a long thinking process, so the inference cost of the collection phase is much larger (9.1: long-context decode is slower and the KV cache larger).
    • The "environment" may be something that really has to run: executing code needs a sandbox, agent tasks need a whole software environment started up. Their timings vary widely, and some hang. The "waiting for the slowest environment" and asynchronous collection of 10.3 are practical problems to deal with here.
    • The same problem has to be sampled many times.

    This kind of training is currently one of the fastest-growing sources of demand for inference compute: the training process itself contains a vast amount of inference.

    This card describes practice around 2025, and the details are still changing quickly. Two surveys (see sources) give a fuller picture.

    An analogyLike a student working through practice problems: no teacher needs to explain how to think at each step, as long as there are answers to check against. When right, dwell on that line of thinking; when wrong, try a different idea next time. With enough problems, effective methods are found on one's own.

    So the advantage of this route is that the reward is cheap and reliable and can be used at scale; the model can learn ways of solving problems nobody has demonstrated. Its boundary is equally clear: it applies only to tasks where "right or wrong can be judged automatically".

  4. Leaving language models aside, look at reinforcement learning's traditional strength.

  5. 3Robots and control: train in simulation, transfer to real machines

    Teaching a robot to walk, grasp and keep its balance is hard to do with hand-written rules, and hard with demonstrations. Reinforcement learning's way is to have thousands upon thousands of virtual robots try things at once in a physics simulation, learn a control policy, and then install it on the real robot. The difficulty is that simulation always differs from reality; the remedy is to scramble the simulation's various parameters at random on purpose during training, so that the policy becomes insensitive to the differences.

    The shape of the problem. Controlling a four-legged robot:

    • State: the angle and speed of each joint, the body's attitude, the command (which way to go, how fast).
    • Action: the target position or torque for each joint's motor, given tens to hundreds of times a second.
    • Reward: points for moving forward at the commanded speed; deductions for falling, excessive joint forces, jittery movement, and using too much energy.

    Actions are continuous and high-dimensional, so the policy route is used (10.2). The policy network is small, an MLP of a few layers, and has to run in real time on the small computer the robot carries.

    Why not learn on the real machine.

    • Slow: the real world can only run at 1× speed. Learning to walk takes hundreds of millions of steps, which at 50 steps a second takes several months.
    • Expensive and dangerous: early policies are random, and the robot keeps falling and damaging itself.
    • Cannot be parallelised: the cost of ten thousand robots is unacceptable.

    In simulation none of these three is a problem. The GPU parallel simulation of 10.3 runs thousands of robots at once on one GPU, cutting the training of a walking policy from days to tens of minutes.

    The gap between simulation and reality. Simulation is an approximation of reality: real motors have latency and friction, the ground varies in softness, the robot's mass distribution differs from the design drawings, and sensors are noisy. A policy that walks very well in simulation may happen to exploit some unrealistic detail of the simulation, and fall over at the first step on the real machine. This too is a kind of hacking (10.1), only of the simulator.

    Domain randomisation. During training, each parallel environment uses slightly different physical parameters: friction coefficients, the masses of the parts, motor strength and latency, unevenness of the ground, a random shove to the robot. The policy has to learn to work under all these variations. The real world is then just one member of this large family of variations. Here the ten thousand parallel environments take on a second meaning: they are not only for speed, but also for variety.

    Other common methods.

    • Calibrate the simulator with data collected on the real machine, narrowing the gap itself.
    • Train in two stages: first a "teacher" policy learns the task in simulation using information not available on the real machine (such as the exact terrain), then a "student" using only real sensors is trained to imitate it.
    • Let the policy infer the characteristics of the current environment from its recent observations, and adapt to them.

    The same method is used elsewhere. Planning for autonomous driving, flight control for drones, dexterous manipulation with robot arms, regulating industrial processes (data centre cooling, chemical processes): all follow the pattern of "train in a simulation or model first, then deploy".

    From the systems point of view. This is the typical "classic case" in 10.3's comparison table: the model is very small, one GPU is enough; the bottleneck is the speed and realism of the simulation; the GPU is both simulator and trainer. Deployment brings another kind of systems problem: the policy has to output actions in real time at a fixed rate on the robot, with stable latency, a close relative of the inference latency of 9.3.

    An analogyLike pilots training in a simulator: a real plane cannot be crashed, while in the simulator it can be crashed ten thousand times. So that the pilot does not panic in a real plane, the simulator deliberately sets all sorts of winds and all sorts of faults. Having seen enough situations, the real one is just one of them.

    So reinforcement learning in robotics is the most direct beneficiary of 10.3's GPU parallel simulation. The rapid progress of the past few years in the locomotion of four-legged and humanoid robots comes mainly from the combination of "large-scale simulation + PPO + randomisation".

  6. Now a few areas where reinforcement learning has long been applied, and the particular traits of each.

  7. 4Games, recommendation, scheduling: a few classic application areas

    Reinforcement learning's best-known results are in games: Go, chess, Atari, StarCraft. Games have clear rules and winners, allow unlimited self-play, and are an ideal testing ground for research. In industry it is used in recommendation systems (optimising long-term user value rather than a single click), resource scheduling, chip layout, ad bidding and more. What these settings share is that decisions take many steps in a row, and there is a way to score the result.

    Games: where the methods were born.

    • Atari (DQN, 10.2): learning control directly from pixels, showing that deep networks can be combined with reinforcement learning.
    • Go (the AlphaGo series): combining learned policy and value networks with tree search. The policy network proposes moves worth considering, the value network evaluates positions, and the search looks many moves ahead on that basis. The training data comes from self-play: playing again and again against its own older versions, with the winning side's moves reinforced. Later versions used no human game records at all, starting from random and surpassing all humans through self-play alone.
    • Complex real-time strategy and multiplayer games: needing long-range planning, incomplete information and cooperation with teammates. These projects were the main drivers of the large-scale distributed architectures of 10.3.

    The value of games is that they are perfect testing grounds: clear rules, extremely fast simulation, winning is the reward, and data can be produced without limit.

    Recommendation systems. Traditional recommendation is supervised learning: predict whether the user will click on this item. It optimises the click at hand. But what a platform really cares about is the long-term outcome: whether users keep finding it worthwhile, whether they come back. Always recommending what is easiest to click on may tire users quickly. Treating recommendation as a multi-step decision problem (the state is the user's history, the action what to recommend, the reward long-term retention and satisfaction) is exactly the framework of reinforcement learning.

    The particular difficulty here is that trial and error cannot be done freely: exploring means recommending possibly unsuitable things to real users. So a great deal of work goes into how to learn from existing log data (offline reinforcement learning), and how to explore a little, safely.

    Scheduling and resource allocation. Deciding which task goes on which machine, how a data centre's cooling is adjusted, how network traffic is distributed. These problems have clear, quantifiable goals (energy use, completion time), the decisions are continuous, and a simulator or historical data is usually available. The cluster scheduling of part 8 is also something people have tried to do with reinforcement learning.

    Chip design. Deciding where each of the thousands of blocks on a chip goes, so that wiring is shortest and timing and power meet their targets. Placing blocks one by one is treated as a string of actions, with the quality of the final layout as the reward.

    Advertising and bidding, financial trading. Making a string of decisions under constraints of budget and risk.

    The traits they share.

    • Decisions are not one-off but a string, with earlier choices affecting later situations.
    • There is a quantifiable final goal, but no saying what the "correct answer" is at each step.
    • There is a way to try at scale: a simulator, self-play, or enough historical data.

    It is also worth seeing where it has not become mainstream. Many problems once thought to suit reinforcement learning were solved in practice by simpler means: supervised learning with good features, or traditional optimisation algorithms. Reinforcement learning's training is unstable, hard to debug and sensitive to reward design, and these costs are worth paying only when other methods truly fall short. The next card makes this judgement clear.

    An analogyGames are to reinforcement learning what fruit flies are to genetics: researchers do not really care about fruit flies, but they breed fast and are easy to observe, and the laws found in them can be used elsewhere.

    So when you hear "reinforcement learning beat the world champion of some game", what deserves attention is the methods in it (self-play, combining search with learning, large-scale parallelism), all of which were later put to use on more practical problems.

  8. Finally, a practical question: meeting a new task, how do you judge whether to use reinforcement learning?

  9. 5What problems suit reinforcement learning

    Problems that suit reinforcement learning have three traits at once: no ready-made correct answer to imitate, but results can be scored; decisions take many steps in a row, with earlier choices affecting later ones; and trial and error can be done at scale at acceptable cost. Without the first, supervised learning is simpler and more reliable; without the second, it is an ordinary optimisation or prediction problem; without the third, reinforcement learning cannot learn.

    Three questions, asked in turn.

    One: is there a correct answer to learn from directly? If there is a lot of (input, correct output) data, or expert demonstrations to imitate, use supervised learning. 10.1 listed its advantages: direct feedback, fixed data, stable training. Only in two situations do you need to go further: there are no demonstrations at all; or you want to surpass the level of the demonstrations.

    Two: are decisions multi-step, with long-term consequences? If each decision is independent and its quality is known at once, this is the bandit problem of 10.1, or an ordinary optimisation problem, and there are simpler methods. The machinery of reinforcement learning (value functions, credit assignment) is there for "choices now affect the situation later".

    Three: can trial and error be done at scale? Reinforcement learning usually needs on the order of millions to billions of interactions. One of the following is needed:

    • a simulator that is fast and realistic enough (games, physics simulation);
    • a program that can judge results automatically (code tests, answer comparison);
    • trial and error that costs very little and carries no danger.

    For problems where every trial in the real world is expensive or dangerous (medical decisions, large financial operations), using reinforcement learning directly is very hard, and can only be done carefully from historical data.

    And one precondition throughout: the reward has to be right. Can you write a reward such that "maximising it" really equals "achieving what you want"? The hacking that kept appearing in 10.1 and 10.4 shows this is harder than it looks. With the wrong reward, even with all three traits met, you get an agent skilled at gaming the system.

    Why "if supervised learning will do, do not use reinforcement learning". Gather up the difficulties of 10.1 to 10.3:

    • Training is unstable, and results vary a lot with the random seed.
    • Sample needs are several orders of magnitude larger.
    • Debugging is hard: when results are poor, it is hard to tell whether the problem is the algorithm, the reward, exploration or the implementation.
    • Designing the reward is itself a craft that takes repeated trying.
    • The system is more complicated (10.3).

    This is also why real systems are often hybrids: first get a decent starting point with supervised learning (imitation), then improve on it with reinforcement learning. The first step of RLHF is supervised fine-tuning; the first version of AlphaGo first imitated human game records; robot policies often first learn an initial version from demonstrations.

    Where it is mainly used now. From this course's point of view, what most deserves attention is its combination with large models. Pretraining gives a model knowledge and language ability, but that is "imitation". Teaching a model to solve problems with no standard solution, to use tools and to complete long tasks needs a way of learning that is "act, then look at the result". This has turned reinforcement learning from a relatively niche field into a fixed stage in the training pipeline of large models, and has joined its systems problems up completely with what the first nine parts of this course covered: what it needs is a cluster that does both inference and training well.

    An analogyLike choosing transport: a plane is powerful, but worth taking only when the distance is long enough, there are airports, and other ways cannot get there or are too slow. To go to the shop downstairs, walking will do.

    So faced with a new task, first ask whether supervised learning can solve it. If it can, do not use reinforcement learning. Reinforcement learning is the choice when "there is no answer to learn from, only results to judge", and today its most important new setting is precisely teaching large models tasks that have no standard solution.

Putting it together

On large models, reinforcement learning is used for alignment (RLHF: learn human preferences as a reward model, then use it to adjust the language model) and for training reasoning ability (when answers can be checked automatically, use right or wrong directly as the reward). On robots, it trains in large-scale parallel simulation and then transfers to real machines. It is also used in games, recommendation, scheduling and chip design. Problems that suit it have three traits: no ready-made correct answer, but results can be scored; decisions take many steps in a row; trial and error can be done at scale.
